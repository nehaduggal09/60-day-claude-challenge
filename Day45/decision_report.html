The Real Decision

The actual decision isn't "which topic sounds good" — it's which topic can become a real paper by Sunday with no working model and no room to redo it. The trade-off is depth versus feasibility: the most interesting topic (a case study with real systems) needs more digging than the safest one (a conceptual framework). It's hard because you're picking under time pressure with a one-shot deadline, not because the topics themselves are hard.

The Case For Each Option

Option A — Conceptual Framework Paper

Strongest case: No model or data collection needed — you can write and defend it purely through argument and structure, which fits a no-model, tight-deadline situation directly.
Hidden upside: Framework papers are easy for a judge to grade fast — clear structure, clear contribution, nothing to fact-check against benchmarks.
Biggest weakness: Can feel thin or too abstract if not anchored in concrete examples.
Best if you value: speed and certainty of completion.

Option B — Comparative Analysis of Green AI Techniques

Strongest case: Uses existing published benchmark numbers, so it looks data-driven without you running experiments.
Hidden upside: Feels more "technical," which can read as more rigorous to a judge.
Biggest weakness: Needs you to find, verify, and reconcile numbers from multiple papers in a few days — more research overhead than A.
Best if you value: technical credibility over speed.

Option C — Case Study on a Real System

Strongest case: Grounded and concrete — judges tend to like a real example (e.g. DistilBERT, MobileNet) they can picture.
Hidden upside: Easiest to make memorable and specific, which helps it stand out.
Biggest weakness: Requires you to deeply understand one system's internals fast — riskiest if you hit a wall partway through.
Best if you value: distinctiveness and are confident researching one system quickly.

Assumption Buster

Assumptions you may be making

  • That a paper without a working model is automatically weaker than one with a model — a judge who explicitly said "no review" is likely open to conceptual contributions.
  • That there's one "perfect" topic out there you haven't found yet — in reality, several of these are workable; the gap is mostly execution time.
  • That picking wrong is catastrophic — you said you can't undo this, but "can't undo the choice of topic" and "can't produce a decent paper" are two different risks.

Biases at play

  • Loss aversion — because you can't redo this, you're weighting the fear of a wrong pick more heavily than the upside of just starting.
  • Analysis paralysis / status quo bias — staying in "searching for a topic" mode feels safer than committing, even though every day spent searching shrinks your writing time.

What you're definitely ignoring

Time itself as a scoring factor — you haven't weighed how many actual working hours each option needs against how many you have before Sunday.

Premortem — 12 Months Later, It Failed

Option A — Framework Paper

  • The framework was too generic and didn't say anything new.
  • No concrete examples to back up the claims, so it read as filler.
  • Judge wanted more technical depth than a pure framework offers.

Early warning sign: by day 2, you still can't state your core argument in one sentence.

Prevention action: write the one-sentence thesis first, before any section, and test it against a friend or senior.

Option C — Case Study

  • Couldn't get deep enough into the system's internals in time.
  • Public data/benchmarks for the chosen system were incomplete.
  • Ran out of time midway, leaving the paper half-finished.

Early warning sign: by day 3, you're still gathering background instead of writing.

Prevention action: pick the system today, cap research to 1 day, and start drafting even if research feels incomplete.
